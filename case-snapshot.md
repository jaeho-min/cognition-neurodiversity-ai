# Case Snapshot 
 
## Historical record 

At 9 years old, I underwent a psychological assessment at a university hospital in South Korea.  

Around 22 years later, after I had already begun noticing changes in how I approached and structured problems, I obtained a hospital-certified copy of that childhood record.  

Then I learned for the first time that I had been diagnosed with ADHD in childhood.   
 
The historical record includes: 
 
· Full Scale IQ: 112 
· Verbal IQ: 118 
· Performance IQ: 103 
· Examiner-estimated intellectual potential: approximately 128+ 
· visual and auditory attention difficulties 
· impulsivity 
· variable response times 
· ADHD diagnostic impression 
 
These are historical findings from childhood, not a description of my current adult cognitive profile. 
 
I have never taken medication for ADHD. 
 
 
## Why publish this? 
 
IQ and diagnostic information are personal. 
 
I am choosing to disclose part of that information because I do not regard an IQ score as an absolute or immutable description of a person. 
 
Cognitive performance can change over time, and a score can be measured again, interpreted in a different context, and compared with other aspects of a person's functioning. 
 
This also led me to think more broadly about different kinds of personal information. 
 
A leaked password can be changed. 
 
A cognitive score can be re-measured, reinterpreted and placed within a different developmental context. 
 
Genetic information is different. 
 
Once genetic information is exposed, you cannot simply change or replace your genome in the way you can replace a password. 
 
That distinction matters to me, and I think the boundary between changeable, revocable and effectively permanent personal information deserves deeper examination. 
 
If I regarded these cognitive measurements as permanent, identity-defining information in the same sense as immutable biological information, I would be much more reluctant to publish them. 
 
Instead, I treat them as historical measurements from one point in a changing system. 
 
For me, the more interesting question is not: 
 
> How intelligent is this person? 
 
It is: 
 
> How is this person's thinking structured, where does friction occur, and can that structure be understood and improved? 
 
 
## The human as a system 
 
I increasingly think of a human being as both part of larger systems and, in some ways, as a system in itself. 
 
This is a working model rather than a claim that human cognition can be reduced to engineering. 
 
But the analogy is useful. 
 
Systems can have: 
 
· constraints 
· dependencies 
· bottlenecks 
· failure modes 
· recovery paths 
· overhead 
 
Cognition may have its own forms of overhead. 
 
For example: 
 
· holding too much context in working memory 
· repeatedly reconstructing information 
· switching between competing thoughts 
· organizing incomplete ideas 
· losing context after interruption 
· repeatedly solving the same organizational problem from scratch 
 
My working hypothesis is that some of this cognitive overhead can be reduced if useful structures are made explicit and repeatable.
